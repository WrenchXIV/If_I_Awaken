¶ 1

A note before the numbers.

This is a long letter. It contains no slides, no photographs, and no music. If that does not interest you, we apologize for taking your time. If it does, what follows is the most direct presentation we can write of what we are building, why now, and the terms on which you might join.

Theatrical investment is not for everyone. The most honest way to discuss it, in our view, is the way Warren Buffett discusses paper mills and railroads: plainly, in complete sentences, with the data first and the persuasion last. This document tries to do that.

We will tell you what we are building (¶8↗), what we have already proven (¶3↗–¶5↗), what the operating model looks like (¶9↗), what could go wrong (¶11↗), and what we are offering (¶13↗). The sequence is intentional; the conclusion is at the end where it should be.

¶ 2

The thesis, in one sentence.

Los Angeles is the largest entertainment market in the world without an iconic immersive show about itself, and the next three years contain the largest sustained cultural-attention event in modern LA history. We propose to occupy that gap.

To be plain: New York has Sleep No More (13+ years across NYC and Shanghai). Santa Fe has Meow Wolf ($300M+ across five US cities). Las Vegas has Cirque (multiple billion-dollar resident shows). Los Angeles — 18 million metropolitan, 50 million-plus annual visitors, the largest entertainment industry in human history — has nothing in the same slot. Not for residents. Not for visitors. Not for the press the city is about to receive.

The form we are building, If I Awaken in Los Angeles, is designed to fit it: a 90-minute walking immersive theatrical experience that places 150 guests per show inside the actual neighborhoods of LA — Hollywood Boulevard, Boyle Heights, the Asian Arc, Black LA across a century, Folk LA, the Stage, the Real LA — with the actual people and the actual music. 30,000 sq ft in the Arts District, next to the 6th Street Bridge. Twelve performances per week. Opening June 2027.

¶ 6

The window.

The next three years are not an ordinary three years for Los Angeles. The FIFA World Cup arrives in 2026; the Super Bowl in 2027; and the Summer Olympics, with the Paralympic Games immediately after, in 2028. Olympic visitor projections from the LA28 organizing committee place unique attendees in the range of 15 million, with media impressions estimated to exceed two billion.

We open in June 2027. That gives the show roughly twelve months to find its rhythm before the Olympic surge arrives — and positions the venue to anchor festival programming, cultural pavilions, and international arts showcases during the surge itself.

The fact is not lost on us that this is the same window the city has been waiting on for forty years. We do not think it should pass without a permanent piece of cultural infrastructure that represents the city to the world.

¶ 8

The show.

One hundred minutes, end-to-end. Seven built environments plus a lobby that doubles as a theatre. 150 guests per show. Twelve performances a week, Tuesday through Sunday. The audience does not sit until the finale. They walk.

The arc, briefly: Hollywood Boulevard (a 110-ft LED wall that pivots), Boyle Heights (a tianguis with stone tables; two poets meeting across a generation), the Asian Arc (Chinatown, Little Tokyo, Koreatown, Historic Filipinotown, Thai Town — five neighborhoods, one rapper-poet walking the line), Black LA across a century (a church wall opening into gospel, swing dance at Dunbar, a hip-hop cypher, a Leimert Park drum circle), Folk LA / Laurel Canyon (a knock at a door becoming a living-room sing-along), the Stage (the only seated room — a classical proscenium that has earned its formality), and the Real LA (a return to the opening corridor, the wall this time made of the audience's own faces).

Each room has its own scenic register, its own cast, its own sound design.

¶ 10

The events business — and why the venue itself is worth more than the show.

The show runs Thursday–Sunday evenings and weekend afternoons. Monday through Wednesday and weekday daytime — approximately 3.5 days per week — the venue sits dark. Across a 60-week opening run, that is ~210 day-equivalents of premium rentable real estate.

The venue, as designed, will have six things most LA venues do not have under one roof:

  • A 110-ft permanent LED wall — by some measure the most dramatic indoor screen in the city.
  • A real bar program (64-ft bar, VIP zone, neon).
  • A 150-seat proscenium theater with a working stage.
  • An outdoor red-carpet courtyard with a marquee — premiere-tier, rare in LA.
  • 30,000 sq ft of versatile space (500+ cocktail-cap).
  • Arts District location — premium real estate for entertainment-industry clients.

¶ 11

What could go wrong.

Theatrical investment carries risk of loss. We list the risks we take most seriously, in order:

  • Build / permit timing. A 30,000 sq ft custom buildout to a fixed opening date is the biggest schedule risk. Mitigation: our GM ran the Coachella, Stagecoach, and Grammys buildouts; the schedule includes two months of contingency before the Olympic surge.
  • Cast retention across an 18-month run. Immersive shows that grind down their performers underdeliver. Mitigation: union scale, swing cast, the rotational programming Scoville used on Here Lies Love.
  • Macro / consumer discretionary softness. Mitigation: the tiered pricing structure (Community $50) keeps the demand floor high; the events business adds a non-ticket revenue line independent of consumer ticket spending.
  • National / international concept fit. An LA-specific story has to land for non-LA visitors. Mitigation: the form's universal frame ("a real city told by its real people") is built into the concept, and the pilot drew critical acclaim from non-LA names (Foster, Hedison).

We are happy to send the full risk register and the operating model with sensitivity tabs on request.

¶ 12

After Los Angeles.

LA is the first chapter. The production architecture — lighting, projection, sound, walk-through staging — is the intellectual property; the local cast and the local truth are what change per city. The marginal CapEx for a second city is materially below the first.

Three candidate next cities, each with the local cultural particulars that make them obvious: Chicago (house music, South Side to lakefront), New York (five boroughs, subway rhythms, diaspora), and London (Brixton to Brick Lane). The comparable franchise — Meow Wolf at $300M+ across five US locations — is the precedent.
